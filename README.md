# Undergraduate-Thesis-Work-
Thesis Title: Comparative Phylogeography of Three Nematode Parasites with Contrasting Life Cycle 
Abstract: 
Parasitic nematodes represent a major component of global biodiversity and exert profound effects on human and animal health, ecosystem dynamics, and host population structure (Chen et al., 2012). Among them, zoonotic and vector-borne helminths such as Toxocara canis, Ascaris lumbricoides, and Dirofilaria immitis are globally distributed and epidemiologically significant. This study employed a bioinformatics approach that repurposes Toxocara canis, Dirofilaria immitis, and Ascaris lumbricoides cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) sequences obtained from GenBank to quantify and compare genetic diversity metrics, population differentiation, and haplotype network patterning across all three species, thereby generating the first direct comparative phylogeographic assessment of these globally important helminths (Fogt-Wyrwas et al., 2019). The contrasting phylogeographic patterns observed among Ascaris lumbricoides, Dirofilaria immitis, and Toxocara canis highlight how transmission mode shapes evolutionary dynamics at a global scale. Global COI analyses revealed contrasting population structures among Ascaris lumbricoides, Dirofilaria immitis, and Toxocara canis, with the former two showing high haplotype but moderate nucleotide diversity, and D. immitis exhibiting fewer yet deeply divergent lineages. Overall, D. immitis populations were more globally intermixed, while A. lumbricoides and T. canis showed stronger regional structuring alongside evidence of gene flow, reflecting differences in transmission dynamics and host movement. Collectively, these findings reinforce the principle that parasite population structure is shaped by the interaction of ecological filtering, host movement, vector biology, and anthropogenic change.
